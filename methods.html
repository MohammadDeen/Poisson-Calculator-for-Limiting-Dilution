Methods & Maths

The equations behind the limiting-dilution Poisson calculator, with derivations, the reverse solve, the seeding-density conversion, and the assumptions they rest on.

1. The Poisson model of cell dispensing

In limiting-dilution cloning, a cell suspension is dispensed into wells so that each well receives a small, random number of cells. If cells are randomly and independently distributed in a well-mixed suspension, the count of cells landing in any one well follows a Poisson distribution with mean \(\lambda\) (the average number of cells deposited per well).

The probability that a given well receives exactly \(k\) cells is the Poisson PMF:

$$ P(k) = \frac{\lambda^{k}\, e^{-\lambda}}{k!}, \qquad k = 0, 1, 2, \dots $$

Here \(\lambda > 0\) is the only parameter. Everything the calculator reports is derived from this single equation.

2. Empty, single, and multi-cell wells

P(0): empty wells

Setting \(k = 0\) and using \(0! = 1\) and \(\lambda^{0} = 1\):

$$ P(0) = \frac{\lambda^{0} e^{-\lambda}}{0!} = e^{-\lambda}. $$

P(1): single-cell (monoclonal) wells

Setting \(k = 1\) with \(1! = 1\):

$$ P(1) = \frac{\lambda^{1} e^{-\lambda}}{1!} = \lambda\, e^{-\lambda}. $$

These are the wells of interest for cloning: exactly one cell, so any resulting colony is presumptively monoclonal.

P(2+): multi-cell wells (contamination risk)

Because the probabilities over all \(k\) must sum to 1, the probability of two or more cells is the complement of the empty and single cases:

$$ P(2^{+}) = 1 - P(0) - P(1) = 1 - e^{-\lambda} - \lambda e^{-\lambda} = 1 - e^{-\lambda}\,(1 + \lambda). $$

Expected single-cell wells on a plate

For a plate (or batch) of \(N\) wells, the expected number of monoclonal wells is simply \(N\) times the single-cell probability:

$$ \mathbb{E}[\text{single-cell wells}] = N \cdot P(1) = N\,\lambda\, e^{-\lambda}. $$

The calculator reports this for \(N = 96\) by default.

Single-cell fraction among occupied wells

At the bench you usually judge clonality from wells that actually grow — i.e. wells that received at least one cell. The relevant quantity is then a conditional probability: given a well is occupied (\(k \ge 1\)), what is the chance it holds exactly one cell? Dividing \(P(1)\) by the probability of being occupied, \(1 - P(0)\):

$$ P(1 \mid k \ge 1) = \frac{P(1)}{1 - P(0)} = \frac{\lambda e^{-\lambda}}{1 - e^{-\lambda}}. $$

This is a clonality-confidence measure. Crucially it decreases as \(\lambda\) rises: for example it is about 85.7% at \(\lambda = 0.3\), 77.1% at \(\lambda = 0.5\), 69.1% at \(\lambda = 0.7\), and 58.2% at \(\lambda = 1\). So while \(\lambda = 1\) maximises the absolute number of single-cell wells, a lower \(\lambda\) gives higher confidence that any given growing well is monoclonal. The calculator shows this as the “single-cell among occupied wells” metric and as a column in the occupancy table.

Usable colonies after plating survival

Poisson describes how cells are distributed, not whether they live. If only a fraction \(S\) of single seeded cells survive and expand into a colony, the expected number of usable monoclonal colonies from \(N\) wells is

$$ \mathbb{E}[\text{usable colonies}] = N \cdot P(1) \cdot S = N\,\lambda e^{-\lambda}\,S. $$

\(S = 1\) (100%) recovers the pure-Poisson upper bound. The Experiment Planner exposes \(S\) so you can plan realistic yields for stressed, CRISPR-edited, or slow-growing lines — for example, 30 expected single-cell wells at \(S = 0.4\) yield only about 12 usable colonies. Note this is a simple scalar correction; it assumes survival is independent of how many cells a well received.

3. Why P(1) peaks at 36.79%

\(P(1) = \lambda e^{-\lambda}\) is maximised where its derivative with respect to \(\lambda\) is zero:

$$ \frac{d}{d\lambda}\big(\lambda e^{-\lambda}\big) = e^{-\lambda} - \lambda e^{-\lambda} = e^{-\lambda}(1 - \lambda) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \lambda = 1. $$

At \(\lambda = 1\), \(P(1) = 1 \cdot e^{-1} = 1/e \approx 0.3679\). So no choice of \(\lambda\) can put more than about 36.79% of wells into the single-cell state. If you ask for a target above this, the tool reports the feasible clipped maximum.

4. Reverse solve: lambda from a desired number of single-cell wells

The Experiment Planner works backwards. Given \(N\) total wells and a desired number of single-cell wells \(s\), the target single-cell probability is

$$ p = \frac{s}{N}, \qquad \text{and we solve} \qquad \lambda\, e^{-\lambda} = p \;\; \text{for } \lambda. $$

This equation has no elementary closed form; it inverts through the Lambert W function, \(\lambda = -W(-p)\), which is two-valued for \(0 < p < 1/e\). There are therefore two roots: a low one with \(\lambda < 1\) and a high one with \(\lambda > 1\).

Because the goal is monoclonality, the planner keeps the low root \((0 < \lambda < 1)\), which minimises the multi-cell contamination \(P(2^{+})\) for the same \(P(1)\). It is found numerically by bisection on \(f(\lambda) = \lambda e^{-\lambda} - p\) over the interval \((0, 1)\). If \(p > 1/e\) the target is infeasible and the tool says so.

5. Seeding-density conversion

\(\lambda\) is an average count per well. To prepare a suspension you need a concentration. If each well is plated with volume \(V\), the required suspension concentration \(c\) satisfies

$$ \lambda = c \cdot V \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad c = \frac{\lambda}{V}. $$

Working in laboratory units — concentration in cells/mL and plating volume in microlitres per well — and using \(1\ \text{mL} = 1000\ \mu\text{L}\):

$$ C_{\text{susp}}\ \left(\tfrac{\text{cells}}{\text{mL}}\right) = \frac{1000\,\lambda}{V\ (\mu\text{L/well})}. $$

The plating volume \(V\) drives this number. The calculator defaults to \(V = 100\ \mu\text{L/well}\). If your protocol plates a different volume (for example \(200\ \mu\text{L}\)), the required concentration changes by exactly that factor — halving it here would leave you seeding twice as many cells per well. Always set \(V\) to match your actual plating volume.

From concentration to pipetting

Given a stock concentration \(C_{\text{stock}}\) and a final prep volume \(V_{\text{final}}\), the dilution factor and the volume of stock to add follow from conservation of cells, \(C_{\text{stock}} \cdot V_{\text{stock}} = C_{\text{susp}} \cdot V_{\text{final}}\):

$$ \text{dilution factor} = \frac{C_{\text{stock}}}{C_{\text{susp}}}, \qquad V_{\text{stock}} = \frac{C_{\text{susp}}\, V_{\text{final}}}{C_{\text{stock}}}, \qquad V_{\text{medium}} = V_{\text{final}} - V_{\text{stock}}. $$

6. Assumptions and limitations

  • Random, independent placement. The Poisson model assumes each cell lands in a well independently of the others, with a uniform average rate. This holds only for a well-mixed, single-cell suspension.
  • Clumping and poor mixing break it. If cells aggregate, settle, or the suspension is not homogeneous, real counts become over-dispersed: you get more empty wells and more multi-cell wells than Poisson predicts, so apparent monoclonality is overstated. Filtering to a single-cell suspension and gentle, thorough mixing matter.
  • No biology after plating. The calculator models only how cells are distributed. It says nothing about post-plating clonal survival, plating efficiency, or growth rate. A well seeded with one cell may yield no colony; \(P(1)\) is an upper bound on usable monoclonal wells, not a guarantee.
  • Idealised counting. Hemocytometer / counter error in estimating \(C_{\text{stock}}\), pipetting error, and edge/evaporation effects are not modelled. Treat the outputs as a starting point and confirm empirically.
  • Single-hit interpretation. This tool is for cloning design. It is not a limiting-dilution assay (LDA) frequency estimator; it does not fit responding-cell frequencies from fraction-of-negative-wells data.
